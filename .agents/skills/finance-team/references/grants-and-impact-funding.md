# Grants and Impact Funding

The companion to the Grants and impact funding section of `SKILL.md`. Read for `/grant`, and for `/review` of a grant budget or application. Before any of this, confirm the venture's impact mechanism actually clears `startup-team`'s four-link SDG test (problem link, user link, metric link, business-model link, in its `references/opportunity-evaluation.md`); without it, every step below is polishing an application that will lose.

## How grant logic differs from investment

An investor buys a share of an uncertain upside and is paid by growth. A funder buys a measurable outcome against a program's thesis and is paid in evidence that the outcome happened. That changes everything downstream:

- **Fit beats ambition.** An investor forgives an off-thesis pitch if the numbers are exciting; a grant reviewer scores against published criteria and cannot. The question is never "is this a good business" but "does this deliver what this call is paying for."
- **The money is restricted.** Grant funds are approved against specific budget lines and usually cannot be moved between them beyond a stated tolerance without written consent. Investment goes into the bank as working capital; a grant goes in with strings attached to each line.
- **The relationship is compliance, not partnership.** After signing, the funder's interest is reports on time, in their format, with receipts. There's no board seat, but there is an audit right.
- **Time cost is the hidden price.** Non-dilutive doesn't mean free. Applications, reporting, and audit prep are founder hours that a solo founder pays out of the same time budget that runs the venture. Price that honestly.

## Screening checklist

Screen against the live call text, not a summary, a listicle, or memory. For each candidate:

1. **Eligibility.** Entity type and registration (CAC-registered limited company, cooperative, NGO, or individual), incorporation age, sector, geography (country, state, or a named region), stage (idea, pilot, revenue), and founder profile (age, gender, nationality, residency). A single hard miss ends the screen; don't argue it in the narrative.
2. **What's funded and what's excluded.** Many programs exclude salaries, capital equipment, rent, working capital, debt repayment, or anything not tied to the funded activity. If the venture's real need is on the excluded list, the grant doesn't solve the problem it was found for.
3. **Ceiling and currency.** The maximum award, the currency it's paid in, and how it lands (a tranche schedule, reimbursement after spend, or an upfront lump). Reimbursement-style grants require the venture to fund the work first, which is a runway question, not a grant question. Convert to the operating currency at a stated rate and date, and note the FX risk on any multi-tranche award (see `references/commodity-and-currency.md`).
4. **Co-funding or matching.** A required own-contribution (cash or in-kind) is a real cost. State it as a number and confirm the cash exists on the runway model.
5. **Deadline and timeline.** Closing date, expected decision date, and expected disbursement date. A grant that pays out after the runway ends is not funding for this runway.
6. **Reporting and audit burden.** Report frequency and format, whether an external audit is required and at whose cost, retention period for receipts, and site-visit or evaluation obligations.
7. **The "reporting costs more than the grant" test.** Estimate founder hours for the application and for every report and audit over the grant's life, at a realistic value of that time, plus any audit fee. If that total approaches the net award (after co-funding), say plainly that the grant is a net loss or a marginal one, and recommend against it unless the non-cash value (credibility, a funder relationship, a signal to investors) is specifically worth it.

Present the screen as a table (see `references/templates.md`) with a Pursue / Marginal / Skip call and one line of reasoning per program.

## Kinds of programs relevant to a Nigerian early-stage venture

Categories are stable; individual calls are not. Name the category, give a couple of widely known examples so the founder knows what to search for, then verify the current call and its eligibility before doing anything else. Never assume a program is open, still exists, or has the same terms as last cycle.

- **Development finance institutions and multilateral programs.** Challenge funds, innovation windows, and technical-assistance grants run by bodies such as the African Development Bank, the World Bank Group and IFC, the UNDP and other UN agencies, and bilateral agencies (USAID, FCDO, GIZ and similar). Typically larger awards, heavier eligibility and reporting, often routed through an implementing partner rather than applied to directly.
- **Foundation programs.** Entrepreneurship and sector-specific programs from foundations such as the Tony Elumelu Foundation, the Mastercard Foundation, and the Gates Foundation, alongside smaller family and corporate foundations. Ceilings vary widely; many combine a grant with training and a fixed cohort calendar.
- **Accelerator and incubator grants.** Equity-free cohort programs (Google for Startups Africa programs, sector accelerators, and university or hub-run incubators) where the grant is bundled with mentorship and often a demo day. Check whether the money is actually equity-free; some "grants" in this category are convertible instruments in disguise, which is `/raise` territory.
- **Government and agency programs.** Nigerian federal and state programs, including those tied to the Nigeria Startup Act's Startup Investment Seed Fund, NITDA's and the Bank of Industry's innovation and MSME windows, and CBN-linked intervention schemes. Eligibility often turns on registration status (the Startup Label, an MSME registration) and on timing; announcements and actual disbursement can be far apart.
- **Corporate challenge funds and prizes.** Innovation challenges from banks, telcos, energy and agribusiness companies, and global corporates with an Africa program. Usually smaller and faster, sometimes with a pilot or procurement relationship attached, which can be worth more than the cash.

Search by the venture's specific mechanism and sector (financial inclusion, agriculture, energy access, health, education, climate), the SDGs it can defensibly claim, and the founder's profile, not by "grants for startups Nigeria." Keep a dated list of what was checked and what it said, so next cycle starts from evidence rather than from scratch.

## Building the budget from the model

The grant budget is a filtered view of the existing model, not a new document. Take the model's cost lines for the funded activity and period, map each to the funder's budget categories (personnel, equipment, travel, services, overhead, or whatever the call uses), and carry the same labeled assumptions across. Then:

- **Fund only what the call funds.** Anything on the exclusion list moves to the co-funding column or out of the budget entirely, with the source of that money named on the runway model.
- **Keep the unit math visible.** Quantity, unit cost, and basis for the unit cost (a quote, an actual past cost, a labeled estimate) for every line. Reviewers check this, and a funder's audit reconciles to it.
- **Overhead follows their rule, not ours.** Use the funder's stated indirect-cost rate or definition; don't add a general contingency line unless the call allows it.
- **Reconcile to the ask.** Line items sum exactly to the requested amount plus co-funding, in the call's currency, at a stated rate and date.
- **Check it against the runway.** Show what the grant does to net burn and runway under two cases: awarded and disbursed on the funder's schedule, and not awarded. The venture should survive the second.

## Impact metrics

Use the five-dimension framing to find where the mechanism actually bites, then express it in the funder's language:

- **Access:** who can now reach a product, service, or market who couldn't before (users onboarded, locations served, first-time users of a formal service).
- **Affordability:** cost to the beneficiary versus the alternative they used before (price paid, savings per transaction, share of income).
- **Reliability:** consistency of the service against the prior baseline (uptime, delivery success, fulfilment time, failure rate).
- **Income and productivity:** money or time gained by the beneficiary (revenue per merchant or farmer, hours saved, yield or throughput).
- **Safety and trust:** reduction in harm, fraud, or loss, and evidence of trust (incident rate, dispute rate, repeat use, verified identity coverage).

Pick the one or two dimensions where the business model itself produces the change, not the one that sounds biggest. Every metric needs a definition, a data source the venture already has (or a stated plan to collect it), a baseline, and a target. Where no baseline exists, say so and propose a baseline study in the first reporting period rather than asserting one. Where the funder publishes an indicator framework or results template, map each metric to their indicator name and unit and report in that language; a reviewer scoring against a results framework should not have to translate. Pull metric definitions and collection plans from `product-team`'s measurement plan so the venture reports what it already tracks. Never invent beneficiary counts, baseline figures, or outcomes.

## The narrative, assembled from what exists

The application narrative is a re-cut of documents that already exist: the problem and user from `product-team`'s spec and validation work, the opportunity and SDG fit from `startup-team`'s evaluation, the traction evidence from the pitch and actual metrics, the team from what's true. Map each section of the call's template to the source document that answers it, adapt the language to the call's criteria and word limits, and mark anything the call asks for that no existing document answers as a gap to fill with real work, not with prose. Have `content-team` polish the writing once the substance is fixed, and pass the assembled draft to `legal-team` only if the application itself contains binding commitments.

## Post-award obligations calendar

An award converts into a dated list of obligations on signature. Extract from the agreement, and put on the same calendar as the tax filings in `references/nigeria-tax.md`: tranche conditions and the evidence each tranche needs; narrative and financial report due dates and their formats; milestone dates the funder will measure against; audit timing, scope, and who pays; site visits or evaluations; any procurement rules (quotes required above a threshold, approved-vendor lists); the closing report and asset-disposal rules at the end; and the receipt-retention period after close. Set a reminder ahead of each date long enough to gather evidence, not just to write the report.

## Grant fund traceability

From the day the money lands, hold it in a way that lets every naira or dollar be traced to an approved budget line. Practically: a separate bank account or, at minimum, a separate ledger with a tag per budget line; every spend recorded against its line with the invoice or receipt attached at the time, not reconstructed later; a running actual-versus-approved view per line so reallocation requests go in before a line is overspent, since most agreements cap movement between lines and require consent; and a monthly reconciliation of the grant account to the ledger. A funder's audit asks exactly "show me this line, and the receipts," and a venture that can answer in an afternoon keeps the funder relationship; one that can't risks clawback. Grant income also has a tax treatment that depends on the entity and the grant's terms; flag it for the accountant rather than assuming it's tax-free.

## What legal-team reviews in the agreement

Before signing, hand the agreement to `legal-team` with the modeled numbers attached. Their review covers: intellectual property terms (whether the funder takes ownership, a licence, or open-access rights over funded work, and whether that conflicts with what the venture needs to own); clawback and repayment triggers, including on unspent funds, missed milestones, or a change of control; publicity and attribution obligations and any restriction on the venture's own communications; exclusivity, non-compete, or "no other funding for the same activity" clauses; reporting and audit rights and how long they run; conflicts with existing or future investors (anti-assignment clauses, restrictions on equity raises, anything an investor's counsel would object to in diligence); governing law and dispute resolution; and termination for convenience on the funder's side and what happens to committed spend. This skill's job stops at "here is what the money and the obligations look like in numbers," not at signing off on the instrument.

## When this needs a professional

A required external audit, grant income tax treatment, any funder inquiry into how funds were spent, and any grant agreement with IP or clawback terms should go to a licensed accountant or lawyer respectively. This skill's role is to arrive with a clean ledger, a reconciled budget, and a clear question.
