# Nigeria Startup Ecosystem Context

Directional grounding, not a citable dataset — figures in this space move meaningfully year to year and even source to source. Verify anything load-bearing (a specific fund's current ticket size, this quarter's funding total) before relying on it for a real decision.

## What "a billion naira" actually means right now

The naira went through a major devaluation following the Central Bank's 2023 foreign-exchange unification reforms — the exchange rate moved from roughly ₦460-800/$1 (pre-reform, varying by which of the old multiple rates is used as reference) to a peak near ₦1,700/$1 in late 2024, before stabilizing somewhat through 2025-2026 to a range roughly ₦1,300-1,450/$1. At a representative current rate of ~₦1,380/$1, ₦1 billion is roughly $725,000 — meaningfully less than the same naira figure would have represented before 2023. **Always state which year/rate a naira target assumes** — "a billion-naira startup" as a revenue or valuation benchmark means something different depending on whether that's being calibrated against 2020 naira or 2026 naira, and treating them as equivalent quietly moves the goalposts.

## Funding climate

Nigerian (and broader African) startup funding went through a real cycle: a 2021 peak (roughly $2.6B in Nigeria alone by some tracking), a sharp correction through 2022-2024 (down to roughly $556M by 2024 on the same tracking), and a recovery through 2025-2026 (roughly $959M in 2025, continuing into 2026). Some sources show Nigeria and Kenya trading the top spot for African VC destination in different recent years — Kenya's 2025 performance was reportedly boosted heavily by energy/mobility deals — so don't assume Nigeria is automatically the largest single destination without checking the current year's numbers specifically.

The character of the current climate matters more than the total figure: investors post-2022 correction are reported to weight demonstrated sales, retention, and repeatable revenue much more heavily than a growth narrative alone — the "raise on a good story" era of 2021 is widely described as over. A credible plan for reaching real revenue matters more to current investors than it did a few years ago.

## Where early funding actually comes from

- **Angel networks** — e.g. Lagos Angel Network and similar structured groups, typically deploying in the $20,000-$100,000 range per deal.
- **Accelerators with capital** — Y Combinator (a globally recognized standard offer, historically around $500,000 for 7% equity, with a growing and credible African track record); sector-specific programs like fintech/proptech-focused accelerator tracks (some backed by international partners) offering funding in the low hundred-thousands.
- **Grants and equity-free funding** — worth checking specifically for social/environmental-impact-angle businesses, which sometimes qualify for non-dilutive funding not available to a pure commercial play. This track is real but only worth pursuing when the venture's impact case actually clears the four-part SDG-fit test in `references/opportunity-evaluation.md` (a real mechanism connecting the business model to a measurable outcome) — a generic impact narrative bolted onto a commercial pitch doesn't qualify and wastes the founder's time chasing it.
- **Nigeria Startup Act ecosystem support** — beyond the tax incentives (covered in `legal-team` and `finance-team`'s references), the Startup Label process itself and NITDA's associated ecosystem programs are worth checking for current funds, competitions, or support specifically tied to labelled-startup status.

Verify current terms directly before treating any of the above as a specific commitment — accelerator terms, fund sizes, and active programs change.

## Real Nigerian exits, as an ambition reference point

Useful as a scale check when calibrating what "big" actually looks like — not a promise of a similar outcome, and figures below should be re-verified before being quoted as current fact in anything investor-facing. Paystack raised roughly a $1.3M seed, then an $8M Series A, before Stripe's 2020 acquisition at $200M+. Interswitch was valued around $1B following Visa's minority investment after Helios Investment Partners' earlier majority stake. MainOne's acquisition by Equinix was reported around a $320M enterprise value. Jumia's 2019 NYSE IPO opened at $14.50/share. IHS Towers' 2021 NYSE listing valued the company at roughly $7B. These span genuinely different business types (fintech infrastructure, payments, telecom infrastructure, e-commerce, tower infrastructure) — the point isn't that any one is a comparable, it's that "big" in this market has taken several real shapes, and a founder's target is worth checking against an outcome of a similar type, not just the biggest number on this list.

## Structural realities that shape what actually works here

- **Distribution is mobile-first, and often beyond just "an app"** — WhatsApp-based and USSD-based interaction patterns reach a meaningfully broader audience than an app-store-only distribution strategy, especially outside the largest cities. One SME-channel-usage snapshot worth treating as directional (re-verify before citing as current): WhatsApp Business leading usage among Nigerian SMEs at roughly 23%, ahead of Facebook (~19%), Instagram (~15%), email (~10%), bulk SMS (~9%), and Twitter/X (~9%) — WhatsApp's lead is about reach and trust, not automatic conversion; it still depends on how the channel is actually used (broadcast vs. real conversation, response time, whether payment/confirmation happens in-thread).
- **Payment infrastructure has real friction** — card penetration is lower than in mature markets; bank transfer and USSD-based payment habits are often more reliable defaults than assuming card-first checkout.
- **Power and connectivity are real constraints**, not solved problems — a product's reliability assumptions should account for intermittent power and variable connectivity as the normal case for a meaningful share of users, not an edge case.
- **Trust-building has a real, often underestimated cost** — especially for anything handling money or health data, overcoming skepticism (of a new brand, of digital financial products generally) is a genuine part of the go-to-market effort, not just a design or marketing polish problem.
- **Reach outside Lagos/Abuja/Port Harcourt is a distinct challenge** from reach within them — a strategy validated in Lagos doesn't automatically transfer to a national audience, and treating "Nigeria" as a single homogeneous market for distribution or pricing purposes is a common oversimplification.
