# Opportunity Evaluation

The "always on the lookout" check — run before committing any of the nine functional teams' real effort to an idea. This operates one level above `product-team`'s `/idea` and `/validate`: those refine a direction already chosen (the right feature set, the right test to run); this decides whether the direction is worth choosing in the first place.

## What to check

**The problem and who has it.** Not "could this be useful to someone" but who specifically has this problem, how often, how badly, and what they do about it today (including doing nothing, or a workaround). A problem nobody currently spends money, time, or real effort working around is a weak signal, however compelling the idea sounds in the abstract.

Test it against five conditions, all of which should hold: **payable** (the pain is big enough that someone would actually pay to remove it), **scalable** (it recurs across enough people to support a real business, not just one household), **urgent** (it matters now, not "someday" — a nice-to-have doesn't force a decision), **an alternative already exists** (people are already working around it somehow, formally or informally — a problem with zero current workaround, paid or improvised, is often a problem nobody's felt strongly enough yet), and **customer-named** (the customer describes it in their own words, not the founder's product vocabulary — if only the founder can articulate the pain, that's worth noticing). A useful shorthand for the urgency test: does the problem show up as pain, cost, risk, delay, waste, or exclusion? If nothing painful actually changes when it goes unsolved, it's probably too weak to build a venture on.

Two more distinctions are worth running every idea through, because founders default to the wrong side of both. **Need vs. want** — a want is the specific thing a customer asks for (a feature, a look, a price point); a need is the underlying job or pain behind it. Customers are reliable narrators of the want and unreliable narrators of the need, so interpreting past the stated request to the job underneath it is the actual work. **Painkiller vs. vitamin** — a vitamin is nice-to-have, easy to postpone, weak urgency; a painkiller is something people are already spending time or money fighting because leaving it unsolved actively costs them. An idea framed entirely in "wouldn't it be nice if..." language is describing a vitamin. That's a flag, not an automatic kill — some vitamins are real businesses — but they need a higher evidence bar (below) before committing real effort, because "nice to have" ideas are much easier to feel falsely optimistic about in conversation.

A problem statement worth testing further can usually compress into: *[Customer] struggles with [problem] when [situation], causing [impact].* If a founder's stated problem doesn't fit something like that — the customer is vague, the impact is unstated, or the sentence is actually describing the solution — that's often the real issue, not the market.

**Judge the evidence, not just the idea.** Two ideas can sound equally compelling in conversation and sit in completely different places in reality — the job here is telling them apart. Weigh whatever evidence actually exists against a rough evidence ladder, ascending in how much it should move a decision: an *opinion* ("that sounds useful") is weakest; an *anecdote* (one person's story) is next; *observed behavior* (someone doing the workaround unprompted) is stronger; *repeated behavior* stronger still; and *money already changing hands* — a paid workaround, a paid pilot, a deposit — is the strongest signal available before a product even exists. Future intent ("I'd definitely use that," "I'd pay for it") sits below all of these: it's cheap to say and a weak predictor, so a founder's excitement about positive interview responses shouldn't substitute for evidence someone actually acted. Name which rung the current evidence sits on rather than treating "we talked to some people" as inherently strong — and notice specifically what kind of workaround people are already paying for, since a paid *consultant, staff member, or service* to handle the problem manually is a stronger signal than a paid *software subscription* (budget is already fully allocated to solving it, not just supplementing a solution).

If a rough intensity read is useful for comparing ideas or triaging within one venture, a 1-5 scale works: 1-2 (no real workaround, no urgency, or mild irritation people tolerate) means the problem or segment likely needs narrowing before more effort goes in; 3 (real, repeated friction people can describe specific incidents of) is worth continued discovery; 4-5 (people already pay, escalate, or commit real time and money to a fix) is strong enough to justify a real MVP test. This is a triage tool, not a scientific instrument — its value is forcing a specific answer to "how strong is this, really" instead of a vague "pretty strong, I think."

**Real addressable opportunity in this market, not a global TAM figure.** A market-size number pulled from a global report rarely reflects Nigerian purchasing power, payment infrastructure, distribution reality, or willingness to pay for a digital product versus an informal alternative. Ground sizing bottom-up rather than top-down: `reachable customers × realistic conversion × price × frequency`, not `population × an assumed 1% penetration` — a bottom-up figure forces every input to have an actual source (a channel's real reach, a comparable's conversion rate, a price customers have already paid something like) instead of being a number that just sounds plausible. Ground the price point in local purchasing power, the reachable audience in actual distribution channels (mobile-first, often WhatsApp/USSD-relevant, not assuming universal reliable broadband or card-payment penetration), and the path to scale in those same constraints.

**Who else is already solving it, and how well.** Existing players (local or international) solving the same problem aren't automatically a reason to avoid an idea — but ignoring them, or assuming a better UI alone is enough to win, is a common and expensive mistake. Understand what makes an incumbent's position defensible versus what's actually just inertia a genuinely better product could break, and hold any claimed "unfair advantage" to the same standard: "we're passionate," "we're first," "we're cheaper," or "we have more features" are not defensible advantages — they erode the moment a competitor copies them or a bigger player decides to compete. A real unfair advantage looks more like: insider knowledge from having lived the problem, genuine founder-market fit (a team an investor or customer would trust specifically because of who they are), personal authority or domain credibility, network effects (the product gets better as more people use it), exclusive access to a distribution channel, supplier, or dataset, a community/tribe that's hard to replicate, or a proprietary data or feedback loop that compounds over time. If a founder can't point to at least one of these, the honest answer is often "not yet defensible" rather than "no advantage" — worth naming as a gap to close, not necessarily a reason to kill the idea outright.

**Why now.** What's changed that makes this the right time — a regulatory shift (the Nigeria Startup Act's incentives, a new data-protection regime creating demand for compliance tooling), a technology shift (AI capability that wasn't previously viable at this cost), an infrastructure shift (payment rails, connectivity), or a behavior shift (a market moving from informal to digital). An idea with no real answer to "why now" is often either too early (the enabling condition hasn't arrived yet) or already crowded (everyone noticed the same opportunity already).

**What would have to be true to reach real scale.** Name the two or three load-bearing assumptions explicitly — the ones that, if wrong, sink the whole opportunity regardless of good execution elsewhere. This is the same discipline `product-team` applies to a single product's assumptions, run here against the market opportunity itself. When more than one assumption is genuinely uncertain, don't treat them as equally worth testing: weigh each on impact (how much sinks if this is wrong) against uncertainty (how little is actually known yet). The ones that are both high-impact and high-uncertainty are what to test first — that's the assumption that could kill the whole idea and nobody's checked it yet. High-impact/low-uncertainty assumptions just need periodic confirmation; low-impact ones, whatever their uncertainty, can wait. The point of naming this explicitly is to stop a founder (or this skill) from spending the first real test on a comfortable, already-half-answered question instead of the genuinely load-bearing one.

**Operational load at scale.** Ask explicitly: can this run with little founder input once it's working, or does it need the founder in every transaction? Some businesses are structurally founder-heavy — every sale is a negotiation, every delivery needs a supplier chased, every customer expects a personal reply — and that's a ceiling on scale and on how many ventures one person can carry, not a detail to fix later. Prefer opportunities where support can be self-serve and automated first, where fulfilment can follow an SOP someone else runs, and where the founder's involvement per customer falls as volume rises. When an idea is founder-heavy by nature, name it, and name what would have to be built or hired (`ops-team`, `dev-team`'s `/operate`) before it stops being one.

**Fit with existing resources and ventures.** Does this genuinely benefit from what's already built (a relationship, a brand, a distribution channel from an existing venture), or does it compete for the same limited founder time and attention with no real synergy? A good idea that fragments focus across too many things can be worse for the whole portfolio than a slightly less exciting idea that compounds with what's already running.

**Impact/SDG fit, when it's genuinely present — not as decoration.** Some ideas create measurable social or environmental outcomes alongside commercial ones (access to a service, affordability, income for informal workers, safety, health outcomes), and in Nigeria that can open a real, distinct funding track — grants, impact-oriented investors, and development-finance programs that a pure commercial play doesn't qualify for (see `references/nigeria-ecosystem.md`). But an SDG label slapped on a pitch without a mechanism behind it convinces nobody and shouldn't move this skill's assessment either. Before treating impact as a real dimension of the opportunity, it should pass a four-part test: a **problem link** (which specific social problem does this reduce), a **user link** (who benefits and how, specifically — not "the community"), a **metric link** (what's actually measurable, not just claimable), and a **business-model link** (does the impact grow *because* the business grows, or does it require separate subsidized effort that fights the business model). When those four hold, name which of the UN Sustainable Development Goals the venture can plausibly move — one or two with a real mechanism, not an aspirational list of six — since a specific, defensible SDG claim is what a genuinely impact-oriented investor or program will actually check.

## What a recommendation actually looks like

Not a score — a clear call: **pursue now** (with the first concrete step, usually a handoff to `product-team`'s `/idea`), **pursue later** (with the specific condition that would make it worth revisiting — a market shift, a resource freed up, a dependency resolved), or **don't pursue** (with the specific reason, stated plainly rather than softened into ambiguity). Every call should be revisitable — state what evidence or change would flip it, rather than presenting it as a permanent verdict.

## Calibrating ambition honestly

"A billion-naira startup" is a real, meaningful target — and also one whose actual dollar-equivalent scale has shifted substantially with the naira's exchange-rate movement since 2023 (see `references/nigeria-ecosystem.md` for current figures). State which currency/year frame is being used for any target, and don't let an old mental model of naira purchasing power quietly inflate or deflate what a given revenue or valuation figure actually represents today. When a comparison to a real Nigerian outcome would sharpen the ambition check, `references/nigeria-ecosystem.md` has a short list of exits with actual figures — useful as a reality check on scale, not as a promise of a similar outcome.
