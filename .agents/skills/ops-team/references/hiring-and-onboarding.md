# Hiring and Onboarding

How to go from "we need someone for this" to a person who is productive and staying, with a process a solo founder can actually run between everything else. `legal-team` owns the contract and whether the person is an employee or a contractor; `security-team` owns access. This file owns everything between the decision to hire and the day the person is fully working, plus the operational side of a departure.

## Before writing anything: is this a hire?

Answer these first, because each changes the process:

- What specific outcomes does this person own in the first 90 days? If they can't be listed, the role isn't defined yet.
- Could this be a contractor, an agency, a part-timer, or automation instead? Ask `legal-team` about classification once the shape is clear; ask `finance-team` what the fully loaded cost does to runway.
- Is it one role or two? A "marketing and customer support and ops" posting attracts nobody who is good at any of them.

## Job description structure that filters

A job description is a filter. The right person should read it and think "that's me"; the wrong person should self-select out before applying. Structure, in this order:

1. **Title and one-line summary.** A real title people search for, and one sentence on what they'll own.
2. **What you'll actually do.** Five to seven concrete outcomes or recurring tasks, in the order they matter. "Respond to customer WhatsApp messages within one hour during 8am to 6pm and log each in the ticket sheet" filters better than "provide excellent customer service".
3. **What a good first 90 days looks like.** Two or three lines. This is the strongest self-selection tool in the posting.
4. **Must-haves.** Three to five, each tied to a task above, each testable in the interview. Skip anything that can be learned in a month.
5. **Nice-to-haves.** At most three, explicitly labelled so they don't scare off good candidates.
6. **How the work happens.** Location (on-site, hybrid, remote), hours, tools, who they report to (the founder), team size (be honest that it's small).
7. **Pay and terms.** State the range. Postings without pay attract people who will leave when they learn it, and waste both sides' time. Note the engagement type only after `legal-team` confirms it.
8. **How to apply.** Ask for one thing that takes effort proportional to the role: a short answer to a role-specific question, a link to work, or a short task. This filters out mass-appliers.

Cut anything that describes the company's ambition rather than the job. Re-read the finished posting asking: which line would make the wrong person leave?

## Where to source in Nigeria

Go where the actual candidates are for this role, and use more than one channel. Typical channels, verify current state with a web search before relying on any:

- **General job boards:** Jobberman, MyJobMag, Hot Nigerian Jobs, and LinkedIn Jobs for professional and office roles. Expect high volume and low signal; the application question does the filtering.
- **Professional networks:** LinkedIn posts from the founder's own profile, plus a direct ask to five people whose judgment the founder trusts. Referrals are the highest-signal channel for a small team.
- **Tech and product roles:** developer and designer communities on Slack or Discord (search for current Nigerian tech communities), Twitter/X where the founder has a following, GitHub or portfolio sites for direct approaches, and alumni groups of local bootcamps and training programmes.
- **Sales, field, and operations roles:** WhatsApp and Telegram groups for the relevant trade or city, industry associations, and referrals from existing suppliers or customers. Word of mouth outperforms boards here.
- **Entry-level and NYSC:** the NYSC placement route for corps members, and university career centres. Confirm with `legal-team` how corps members and interns should be engaged and paid.
- **Freelance and contract:** Upwork and Fiverr for global talent, plus local freelance networks, when the work is project-shaped. `legal-team` confirms the contractor arrangement.
- **Agencies:** recruitment agencies for senior or hard-to-fill roles, at a fee `finance-team` should approve first.

Post the same JD everywhere, track which channel each shortlisted candidate came from, and reuse the channels that produced the shortlist next time.

## Structured interview design

Decide the structure before the first interview and use the same one for every candidate, so the decision is made against the structure and not on how the founder felt on the day.

**Stage 1: Screen (15 to 20 minutes, call or chat).** Confirm the basics: availability, location and commute, pay expectation against the range, notice period, and one question that checks the top must-have. Drop anyone who fails a hard constraint here; it's kinder than a full loop.

**Stage 2: Work sample (the core of the process).** Give a task that resembles a real week of the job, small enough to take one to three hours, with the materials they'd actually have. Examples: a support hire gets five real (anonymised) customer messages to respond to and categorise; a sales hire gets a prospect brief and writes the outreach plus handles a written objection; a developer gets a small bug or feature in a sandbox repo; an ops hire gets a messy supplier situation to untangle into a plan. Pay for anything longer than a couple of hours. Score against a rubric written before seeing any submissions.

**Stage 3: Role-specific interview (45 to 60 minutes).** Walk through the work sample first ("why did you do it this way, what would you change with more time"), then four to six behavioural questions tied to the must-haves, each asked as "tell me about a specific time when…", pushing for what they personally did and what happened. Add one question on how they handle a situation this venture actually faces (an angry customer, a supplier who missed a delivery, a deploy that broke). Leave ten minutes for their questions; what they ask is signal.

**Stage 4: Reference checks and offer.** Below.

**Scorecard.** One sheet per candidate, filled in immediately after each stage. Rows: each must-have, the work-sample rubric items, communication, and "would I want to work with this person daily". Score 1 to 4 (4 means clearly exceeds; 2 means not there) with one line of evidence per score. No evidence, no score. Format in `templates.md`.

## Reference check questions

Do at least two, one being a former direct manager where possible, by phone or voice note rather than text so tone comes through. Ask:

- In what capacity did you work with [name] and for how long?
- What were they responsible for, and how did they do against that?
- Give me an example of something they handled well without being asked.
- What did they need the most support or supervision with?
- How did they handle a mistake or a disagreement?
- Would you hire them again for the same role? For a bigger one?
- Is there anything I should know before working with them?

Listen for hesitation and for what isn't said. Verify the referee is real (a company line, a LinkedIn profile that matches). Confirm any qualification the role genuinely depends on with the issuing institution.

## Decision process

1. Complete every scorecard before comparing candidates.
2. Rank by must-haves first, then work sample, then everything else. A charming candidate with a weak work sample is a no.
3. Any 2 or below on a must-have needs a written reason to proceed.
4. Decide within a week of the last interview and tell every interviewed candidate the outcome, including the nos. Nigerian professional circles are small; how candidates are treated is remembered.
5. Send a written offer summary (role, pay, start date, engagement type, probation period) and hand off to `legal-team` for the actual agreement and classification. Nothing is signed by ops; nothing starts before `legal-team`'s document is signed.
6. Send `security-team` the access list (below) before day one so provisioning is ready, not improvised.

## 30/60/90 onboarding plan

Write the plan before they start and share it on day one. Full format in `templates.md`.

**Before day one:** signed agreement (`legal-team`), accounts and access provisioned to the minimum the role needs (`security-team`), equipment or a stipend decided, a written brief of the venture (what it sells, to whom, how money comes in, the current top three problems), and their first-week tasks written down.

**Days 1 to 30: learn and do small real work.** Goal: they understand how the venture works and have shipped something small but real. Contents: shadow the founder on the role's core loop, read the relevant SOPs, own one bounded task by the end of week one, own a recurring task by the end of week two, weekly written check-in. Success looks like: they can explain the customer and the core loop in their own words, and the founder has stopped doing the first recurring task.

**Days 31 to 60: own the role's core.** Goal: they run the role's main loop with the founder reviewing outputs, not doing them. Contents: full ownership of the primary responsibility, first improvement suggestion to a SOP, direct contact with the customers or suppliers they'll deal with. Success looks like: the founder checks in on outcomes, not tasks.

**Days 61 to 90: own outcomes and improve.** Goal: they are accountable for the outcomes in the job description with minimal supervision. Contents: own a metric, propose and run one improvement, document one process they now know better than the founder. Success looks like: the 90-day outcomes in the JD are met or clearly on track, and the probation decision is easy.

## Early check-ins

Don't wait for a formal review to surface a problem visible in week two. Hold short check-ins at the end of week one, week two, and then every two weeks through day 90. Each one asks the same four questions: what's going well, what's confusing or blocked, what do you need from me, and one thing the founder has noticed (specific, with an example). Write two lines down after each. If a must-have looks absent by day 30, say so directly, agree what "on track" looks like by day 45, and involve `legal-team` early on how probation works under the agreement. Ending a bad fit inside probation, kindly and clearly, is better for both sides than months of hoping.

## Offboarding checklist (operational side)

Run this as a checklist the day the departure is confirmed, whether they resigned or were let go. `legal-team` handles the employment side (notice, final pay, any dispute); `security-team` handles revocation; this owns the rest, and the three should close on the same timeline.

- [ ] Agree the last working day and communicate it to the team and to any customers or suppliers who dealt with them directly, with the new contact.
- [ ] Handover document: every recurring task they own, its current state, where the files live, and any open promises made to customers or suppliers. Review it together before they leave.
- [ ] Reassign each recurring task to a named person (often the founder, temporarily) and update the relevant SOPs.
- [ ] Send `security-team` the full list of systems they touched, including shared numbers, WhatsApp Business access, and any device with company data, so nothing is missed in revocation.
- [ ] Recover equipment, SIM cards, keys, ID cards, and any stock or petty cash held. Sign a return receipt both ways.
- [ ] Ask `legal-team` to confirm final pay, outstanding leave, and any paperwork before the last day.
- [ ] Short exit conversation: what would have made you stay, what should the next person know. Log it.
- [ ] Update the org list, the on-call or support rota, and any customer-facing "contact" page or message.
- [ ] Note what the departure revealed (a process only they knew, a single point of failure) and open a `/sop` for it.
