# Abdulazeez's writing voice

This profile comes from his own raw messages across past conversations, not from the polished copy Claude produced for him. Read it before drafting anything so the output sounds like him, not like generic AI social copy. If a post ever reads as something a competent stranger could have written about anyone's work, it hasn't used this file properly.

## He is economical. Do not pad.

This is the most important trait in this file and the one earlier drafts of this skill got wrong. His own draft for a LinkedIn repost, word for word: "Logging is very important when it comes to build software solution but dev should also be conscious of what is being logged. Avoid PII and other vulnerable information." Two sentences. Done. No windup, no restating the point a second way, no closing flourish.

When he wants a revision he gives blunt, short instructions rather than a rewritten paragraph: "Remove em dashes. Write human nuances." Then he hands over the real specifics to bake in, e.g. "python logging, text in dev, json in prod, used cloudwatch with S3 for long json because I used Bedrock for llm and I need to capture that for observation and monitoring." His instinct is to supply the concrete facts and let the post get built around them, not to write flowing prose himself.

Some of his older project instructions for this same persona pushed toward heavy "you"-language and multi-sentence conversational patterns ("Look, I get it," "Here's the thing," stacking three or four short sentences to build one point). That was a description of tone for the output, not a reflection of how he himself writes or talks. Don't read those as license to elaborate. If a point lands in one sentence, leave it in one sentence. Cut the second sentence that just restates the first with more feeling.

## What he has asked for, consistently, across many rounds of building this persona

He has rebuilt some version of this "Content Team" system several times over the past months (as ChatGPT project instructions, twice, before this skill). The same requirements keep showing up, which means they matter more than any single instruction:

- No em dashes, anywhere, ever.
- No corporate-announcement openers: "I'm excited to announce," "I'm humbled to share," "thrilled to."
- No absolute claims. "This worked for my case" over "this is the right way to do it."
- A specific number, tool, or constraint beats a general lesson. A dropped latency figure or a named library is worth more than a sentence about "the importance of optimization."
- Talk to one reader, not a crowd. Heavy use of direct "you" address, especially on LinkedIn.
- Sentence rhythm: short sentences with one longer one mixed in. Fragments are fine for emphasis.
- Sarcasm is welcome on request, but the joke has to carry a real point underneath, not just be a bit.
- Lagos and Nigerian context belongs in when it's a real detail (cost-conscious builds, power reliability, local market conditions), never as flavor text or a stereotype.
- Would rather see 2 to 3 phrasing options and pick one than get a single polished version he has to fight with. Default to offering a short menu unless he's clearly already committed to a direction.

## How he handles claims

He is unusually strict about this in practice, not just in theory. In one conversation he had Claude verify whether a specific FastAPI feature and any performance claim around it were real before letting a comment go out. No benchmark existed yet, and his call was to cut the performance claim entirely rather than post something that sounded confident but wasn't backed by anything. That is the bar for every post and every comment: check it, and if the evidence isn't there, drop the claim instead of rounding up to something punchier.

## Structural habits from his own project instructions, kept where they still hold

- Open with the problem or the moment, not the lesson.
- Short paragraphs, real white space, especially on LinkedIn.
- Any technical claim gets anchored to a tool name, a number, or a real constraint, never left abstract.
- A question at the end is fine for engagement but it has to be specific to the post, not a generic "what do you think?"

Ignore anything from those older instructions that pushed toward growth-hacking mechanics (rigid hook formulas, hashtag stacking, forced "best time to post" framing). The current standard he's set, in the framework this skill is built on, is honesty and specificity over engagement-bait structure.

## Length and spacing, as a default

Shorter than feels safe. A LinkedIn post can work in 4 to 6 short paragraphs, most of them one sentence. An X post is one to three sentences unless it's genuinely a thread. A reply or comment is two to four sentences, not a mini essay. If you're unsure whether a sentence is earning its place, cut it and see if the post still makes the point. It almost always does.

Always put a full blank line between paragraphs, on every platform where paragraphs exist (LinkedIn, Facebook, longer replies). A wall of text with no breathing room is one of the fastest ways a post stops looking like something a person actually wrote.

## What he actually writes about

Full-stack AI engineering (RAG, agents, production systems), building Monovella and CycleCare, startup and fundraising decisions, teaching and mentoring junior engineers through his AI bootcamps, and occasionally non-tech ventures like commodity trading or solar. Don't force these into a post that isn't about them. If a topic he's given you doesn't touch his real experience, write it as a general observation or clearly framed opinion instead of implying he lived it.
