# Sales Frameworks

Frameworks are checklists and question structures, not scripts to march through regardless of context. Use the principles behind each to know what to ask and why; pick based on deal size, complexity, and how educated the buyer already is — not out of habit.

## Qualification frameworks

**BANT** (Budget, Authority, Need, Timing) — the simplest starting framework. Good for shorter cycles and lower-complexity deals where a quick go/no-go read is enough. Weak on its own for multi-stakeholder enterprise deals, where "authority" isn't one person.

**CHAMP** (Challenges, Authority, Money, Prioritization) — leads with the buyer's actual challenge rather than budget, which makes it a slightly more consultative starting point than BANT. A good fit for reps or teams newer to structured qualification, or lighter-weight deals.

**MEDDIC** (Metrics, Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, Decision Process, Identify Pain, Champion) — the standard for complex B2B/enterprise sales. Forces specificity: a real metric the buyer cares about, the actual person who can approve spending, their stated decision criteria and process, the pain in their words, and a champion inside the account who will sell on your behalf when you're not in the room.

**MEDDICC** — MEDDIC plus **Competition**: what alternative (including "do nothing" or "build in-house") the buyer is weighing you against, addressed proactively rather than only when it comes up.

**MEDDPICC** — MEDDICC plus **Paper Process**: the legal, procurement, security-review, and contracting steps that can add weeks to a close if not mapped early. Use MEDDPICC for larger deals (roughly six figures and up), longer cycles, and multiple stakeholders, where procurement friction is a real risk to the close date, not just the sale itself.

Rule of thumb: match the framework's weight to the deal's actual complexity. Running full MEDDPICC discipline on a small, single-stakeholder deal wastes time; running BANT on a six-stakeholder enterprise deal misses the reasons deals actually stall (a missing champion, an unmapped procurement step).

## Sales-motion / discovery frameworks

**SPIN Selling** (Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff questions) — a question sequence for surfacing a problem the buyer hasn't fully articulated yet, and letting them connect it to business impact themselves rather than being told. Strongest in discovery; less a full sales strategy on its own. Situation questions gather context; Problem questions surface difficulties; Implication questions build the real cost of the problem; Need-payoff questions get the buyer to state the value of solving it in their own words.

**Gap Selling** — maps the buyer's current state against their desired future state; the gap between the two is the motivator. Simple to explain, and works well combined with SPIN-style questioning to define both states concretely.

**Challenger Sale** — leads with a taught insight that reframes how the buyer sees their own situation, then ties that reframe to your specific differentiator. Effective with buyers who've already done their own research and don't need to be told what your product does — they need a reason your differentiator actually matters to them. Requires real business acumen and credibility to pull off; done badly, it reads as condescending rather than insightful. Weak with buyers who are insecure about their internal standing and don't want to be "challenged."

**Consultative / Value-based selling** — position as the expert helping the buyer make a good decision (which may sometimes mean recommending against your own product), and quantify the financial impact of the outcome in the buyer's own numbers wherever possible. Often paired with Challenger (teach the insight, then prove the value in dollars) or with MEDDIC's Metrics element.

Practical pairing: use SPIN or Gap Selling in discovery to surface and quantify the problem, MEDDIC/MEDDPICC to qualify and track the deal, and Challenger or Consultative framing in the pitch itself.

## Selling in trust-tiered Nigerian channels

In the Nigerian market, the channel a buyer is reached through carries more of the trust than the message does. A cold digital message from an unknown company starts with near-zero trust; the same offer through someone the buyer already knows starts most of the way to a meeting. Match the motion to the channel's starting trust rather than running one script everywhere:

- **Referrals and associations** (an existing customer, a trade or professional association, a religious or alumni network, an industry group's leadership). Highest trust, slowest to build, and the buyer is really buying the referrer's judgment. Motion: ask the referrer for a specific introduction, keep the first message short because the trust is already there, and protect the referrer — an over-promise here costs the relationship that made the sale, not just the deal.
- **Field and agent sales** (in-person visits, distributors, commissioned agents, market and cluster associations). Trust is built face to face, and often the agent is trusted more than the brand. Motion: equip agents with an honest script and the real limits of the offer, pay on retained rather than signed customers where possible, and treat the agent's reputation as part of the product — a mis-sold customer blames the agent, and the agent stops selling.
- **WhatsApp communities and groups** (industry groups, alumni groups, estate or cooperative groups). Medium trust, borrowed from the group's admin and norms. Motion: be a useful member before making any ask, get the admin's permission, and make one clear offer rather than repeated pitches — a group that flags you as a spammer is gone for good, and word travels between groups.
- **Cold digital** (email, LinkedIn, cold WhatsApp, cold calls). Lowest starting trust, cheapest to scale, and the only tier where the compliance rules in `references/outreach-and-ethics.md` are the main constraint. Motion: a specific, true reason to reach out, real proof, and a next step that costs the buyer nothing — and expect the job of a cold message to be earning a move up a tier (a referral, a call) rather than a close.

Two rules that follow from this. First, sequence upward: a founder with no pipeline should exhaust referrals and associations before spending on cold digital, because one warm introduction usually outperforms a large cold send and doesn't burn the market. Second, don't let trust substitute for qualification — a referred prospect still needs the problem, authority, and budget checks above, and a relationship-driven "yes" that isn't backed by a real need becomes a churned account and an awkward conversation with the person who referred them.

## Copywriting frameworks (for outreach, offers, and landing copy)

- **AIDA** (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action) — a general-purpose structure for anything competing for attention cold.
- **PAS** (Problem, Agitate, Solution) — surfaces the problem, makes its cost concrete, then presents the fix. Effective when the buyer doesn't yet feel the problem's weight; overused or run too hard, it reads as manufactured urgency — keep the "agitate" step honest (real consequences, not manufactured fear).
- **Before-After-Bridge** — paints the current state, the improved state, and the offer as the bridge between them. Useful when the after-state is easy to make vivid and true.

Use exactly one framework per piece of copy, chosen for what the specific message needs to do — don't force a formula's full sequence into a two-line follow-up email where it adds structure but not clarity.
