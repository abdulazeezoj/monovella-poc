# Negotiation and Objection Handling

## Principled negotiation and BATNA

The Harvard model (Fisher, Ury, and Patton's "Getting to Yes") is the standard framework for negotiating without either caving or turning it adversarial. Four pieces:

1. **Separate the people from the problem.** Keep relationship/emotional friction distinct from the substance being negotiated — a tense moment over price shouldn't become a tense relationship.
2. **Focus on interests, not positions.** A stated position ("we need a 20% discount") usually sits on top of a real interest (budget cycle timing, a need to show ROI to their boss, a genuine price-sensitivity constraint). Negotiating the interest usually opens up options a fight over the position doesn't.
3. **Invent options for mutual gain before deciding.** Generate multiple possible packages (price/scope/term/payment-timing combinations) before committing to one — this is where trading, not just discounting, comes from.
4. **Insist on objective criteria.** Anchor discussion to something external and defensible (market rate, a documented cost basis, a comparable deal) rather than pure back-and-forth on what each side wants.

**BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement)** — know, before the conversation starts, what you'll do if this deal doesn't happen. That's the real threshold for judging any offer on the table: an agreement only makes sense if it beats your BATNA. On the sell side, a strong BATNA (other real pipeline, no desperate need for this specific deal) gives room to hold firm on value; a weak one is exactly when the temptation to discount reflexively shows up — recognize that pressure for what it is rather than letting it drive the number.

**Anchoring** — the first number stated pulls the rest of the negotiation toward it. Anchor with a defensible number tied to value or market data, not an arbitrary one; when the other side anchors first, don't silently accept their frame — restate value before countering.

**Trading, not discounting.** A discount given for nothing trains the buyer to expect the next one too. Trade specific terms deliberately: a lower price for a longer term, a faster close, a case study/reference right, upfront payment, or reduced scope — always get something back that has real value to your side, even if it's not cash.

## Objection-handling framework

Diagnose which category an objection actually falls into before responding — the same words ("it's too expensive") can mean different things depending on the category, and answering the wrong one wastes the conversation:

- **Fit** — the product genuinely doesn't solve their problem, or solves a different one. Don't argue past a real fit gap; either find the part that does fit or acknowledge it doesn't and part on good terms.
- **Trust** — they don't yet believe the claims (you, the company, or the product). Address with evidence, references, or a smaller first step (pilot, trial) rather than more assertion.
- **Proof** — they believe the claim is plausible but want to see it demonstrated for their specific case. Give a concrete example, a relevant case, or a way to verify it themselves.
- **Timing** — genuinely not the right moment (budget cycle, competing priority, org change in progress). Respect it, stay in touch with real value rather than nagging, and check back at a stated, reasonable interval.
- **Authority** — talking to someone who can't approve the decision. Find the real economic buyer and involve them, rather than continuing to sell to someone who can't say yes.
- **Budget** — a real budget constraint, not a proxy for something else. Explore scope/phasing options honestly; don't discount reflexively without understanding the actual constraint.
- **Risk** — concern about switching cost, implementation risk, or downside if it doesn't work. Address directly: what's the actual failure mode, and what mitigates it (trial period, phased rollout, reference customer in a similar situation).
- **Competition** — comparing against a real alternative, including "build it ourselves" or "do nothing." Understand what they value in the alternative before arguing against it; never claim a competitor weakness you can't actually defend.
- **Misunderstanding** — they're objecting to something the product/offer doesn't actually do or cost. Simple to fix once correctly diagnosed — the risk is treating it as a "real" objection and over-arguing a point that was never true.

In every case: answer honestly, and don't pressure a prospect past a concern that's actually valid. An objection that's true doesn't stop being true because it was handled smoothly — the goal is a clear, honest answer, not a scripted rebuttal that makes the concern disappear from the conversation without actually being resolved.
